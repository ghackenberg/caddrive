\begin{abstract}
Managing variability in mechatronic systems requires a tight coupling between logical feature models and physical geometric assets. However, an "Agile Infrastructure Gap" exists between lightweight early-phase design and heavy industrial Product Lifecycle Management (PLM) solutions. This paper presents a novel three-tier architectural pattern for embedding variability management directly into the solution space using the open LDraw file format. By enforcing a formal separation between Configurations (CT), Features (FT), and Parts (PT) within a single file, we enable a self-contained 150\% model. This approach utilizes a systematic namespace-based naming convention and hierarchical references to encode configuration rules and complex boolean constraints, ensuring maximum portability across CAD editors. We validate the pattern through an FPV drone product line, demonstrating its ability to maintain structural rigor and automate variant derivation.

\keywords{Product Line Engineering \and Mechatronics \and 3-Tier Architecture \and Digital LEGO \and Variability Modeling \and Baukasten.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The engineering of modern mechatronic systems is increasingly defined by the transition from single-product development to the management of complex product families. This shift is driven by the global trend toward mass customization, where manufacturers must offer a wide variety of product configurations while maintaining cost efficiency. To manage this diversity, industry leaders have long relied on the "Baukasten" (Modular Platform) principle \cite{VolkswagenMQB}, sharing core assets across diverse models.

In the mature stages of development, this complexity is managed by enterprise-grade Product Lifecycle Management (PLM) and Product Configuration systems. However, during the early, agile phases of design and in educational settings, these industrial tools are often prohibitively expensive and rigid for rapid experimentation. This creates an "Agile Infrastructure Gap": a lack of lightweight, portable tools that provide the same structural rigor as high-end PLM systems for managing mechatronic variability.

A primary technical challenge within this gap is the persistent disconnect between the problem space (logical feature trees) and the solution space (3D models). Traditional Software Product Line Engineering (SPLE) \cite{Pohl2005} methodologies often treat these as separate artifacts. For physical products, this separation is problematic, as it obscures critical geometric interdependencies, such as space envelopes and component collisions, which are essential for validating physical feasibility.

This paper addresses the Agile Infrastructure Gap by introducing a formal three-tier architectural pattern for embedding variability management directly into the CAD data structure. Our approach, the Unified Variability Representation (UVR), integrates the traditional PLM stack into a single, portable LDraw file. By leveraging a systematic naming convention, we allow the 3D geometry to carry its own variability logic, making the model self-contained and machine-interpretable.

\section{Related Work}

The foundations of variability management were established by the SPLE community, with works by Pohl et al. \cite{Pohl2005} and Linden et al. \cite{Linden2007} defining domain and application engineering. Recently, the Universal Variability Language (UVL) \cite{Benavides2025UVL} has provided a standardized exchange format for feature models, but it remains focused on the abstract problem space.

In mechatronics, research has explored formalizing geometric constraints through SysML profiles \cite{warniez2014sysml} or integrating feature-based modeling with technical systems \cite{Koch2016}. While these provide technical rigor, they often require specialized toolchains disconnected from visual 3D representations. Furthermore, as noted by Kuiter et al. \cite{KuiterTK25}, there is a growing need for pedagogical frameworks that make abstract variability concepts tangible. Our work bridges this gap by extending the open-source CADdrive platform \cite{Hackenberg2023} with a robust, portable mechanism for variant-rich hardware systems, suitable for both agile prototyping and mechatronic education.

\section{The Three-Tier Architecture for Embedded PLE}

We organize the 150\% model into three distinct tiers using a namespace-based prefix system within a single LDraw hierarchy. This structure ensures a clear separation of concerns between logical definitions and physical geometry.

\subsection{Architectural Layers}

The hierarchy is split into three primary functional branches (see Fig. \ref{fig:3_tier_arch}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Configuration Tier (CT):} This layer serves as the "product map." It defines officially supported variants as empty submodels where the variant's name carries the selection logic. This allows the file to act as its own catalog, identifying which combinations of features constitute valid end products.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: Feature Tier (FT):} This layer implements the variability tree and logical constraints. It acts as the "intelligence" of the model, defining mandatory features, selection groups (XOR/OR), and complex boolean dependencies. FT nodes do not contain geometry themselves but reference assets in Tier 3.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3: Part Tier (PT):} This layer contains the logic-free "Baukasten" assets. These are the atomic geometric primitives, such as bricks, motors, or screws, designed for maximum reuse across the different logical features in Tier 2.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Systematic Naming Convention}

To enable machine-interpretability and maintain a clean model structure in standard CAD editors, UVR utilizes a systematic namespace-based naming convention. This convention allows the derivation algorithm to identify the functional role of each submodel definition simply by parsing its name string.

\subsubsection{Global Namespace Prefixes}
Every submodel in the LDraw definition list must start with a tier-specific prefix to establish its scope:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ct\_}: \textbf{Configuration Tier Namespace.} Reserved for preconfigured product variants.
    \item \texttt{ft\_}: \textbf{Feature Tier Namespace.} Reserved for logical variability nodes and constraints.
    \item \texttt{pt\_}: \textbf{Part Tier Namespace.} Reserved for atomic mechatronic assets and raw geometry.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Tier 1: Configuration Syntax (CT)}
Variants in the Configuration Tier identify officially supported product versions. Their name serves as a "Deployment Descriptor" using the following syntax:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Syntax:} \texttt{ct\_[VariantName] ([Formula])}
    \item \textbf{Formula Logic:} A simple additive string of the required concrete features (e.g., \texttt{ct\_base (ft\_c\_part\_a + ft\_c\_part\_b)}). This formula acts as the "bill of features" for the derivation algorithm.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Tier 2: Feature \& Constraint Syntax (FT)}
The Feature Tier implements the variability logic using an Orthogonal Variability Model (OVM) matrix. The labels follow a strict \texttt{[Presence][Nature][\_Logic]} pattern:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Presence dimension:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{m} (Mandatory): Must be included if the parent node is resolved.
        \item \texttt{o} (Optional): May be included or excluded by the user/formula.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nature dimension:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{a} (Abstract): Acts as a Variation Point (VP); defines logic but contains no geometry.
        \item \texttt{c} (Concrete): Acts as a Variant (V); instantiates physical geometry from Tier 3.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Logic dimension (for groups):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{xor}: Exactly one child must be selected.
        \item \texttt{or}: At least one child must be selected.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Tree Constraints:} Complex logic is stored in empty submodels with the \texttt{ft\_cst\_} prefix.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Syntax:} \texttt{ft\_cst\_[Name] ([BooleanExpression])}
        \item \textbf{Operators:} Supports standard logic: \texttt{!} (NOT), \texttt{\&} (AND), \texttt{|} (OR), and \texttt{->} (IMPLIES).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Tier 3: Part Syntax (PT)}
The Part Tier represents the physical asset base (the "Baukasten"). This tier is intentionally logic-free to ensure that assets remain reusable across different product lines and logic models.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Syntax:} \texttt{pt\_[Description]}
    \item \textbf{Logic:} No variability logic is permitted in the Part namespace. Submodels in this tier contain only raw LDraw geometric primitives or mechatronic CAD data. By decoupling the geometry from the logic, mechatronic engineers can update a part (e.g., \texttt{pt\_m2\_screw}) without affecting the underlying variability model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Derivation Algorithm}

The automated variant derivation algorithm ensures that 3D product models are generated subtractively from the 150\% model:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parse hierarchy:} The algorithm builds an internal graph of the unified CAD file, representing submodel definitions and their reference edges.
    \item \textbf{Identify variant:} It locates the requested \texttt{ct\_} submodel definition and extracts the configuration formula from its name.
    \item \textbf{Resolve logic:} Starting from the root, the algorithm recursively marks nodes as "Active" if they are mandatory, explicitly listed in the formula, or required abstract parents.
    \item \textbf{Validate constraints:} Every \texttt{ft\_cst} boolean expression is evaluated. If any evaluates to \texttt{FALSE}, the variant is flagged as invalid.
    \item \textbf{Prune and export:} All non-active nodes are removed, and the final filtered result is exported as a standard 3D model file.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Case Study: FPV Drone Product Line}

We applied UVR to an FPV drone product line to validate the pattern's ability to manage complex physical interdependencies. FPV drones are highly suitable because they are inherently modular but exhibit severe geometric interdependencies.

\subsection{Constraint Identification}

Spatial analysis identified two primary constraint classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Static Constraints:} The \textit{Small Frame} compartment is physically too small for the \textit{Large Battery}, creating a hard geometric incompatibility.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Constraints:} \textit{Large Propellers} have a rotation arc that would collide with the arms of a \textit{Small Frame} during high-speed flight.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Applying the 3-Tier Mapping}

The drone product line is organized into the following hierarchical namespace:
\begin{verbatim}
ct_root
  ct_ultralight (ft_c_frame_small + ft_c_batt_small + ...)
  ct_freestyle  (ft_c_frame_large + ft_c_batt_med + ...)
ft_root
  ft_constraints
    ft_cst_collision (ft_c_frame_small -> !ft_c_large_prop)
  ft_features
    ft_ma_xor_battery_size
      ft_c_batt_small -> References pt_lipo_450mah
      ft_c_batt_large -> References pt_lipo_1300mah
    ft_ma_xor_frame_size
      ft_c_frame_small -> References pt_arm_small
pt_root
  pt_lipo_450mah (Geometry)
  pt_arm_small   (Geometry)
  pt_m2_screw    (Reusable Asset)
\end{verbatim}

\section{Evaluation and Discussion}

The Three-Tier UVR pattern addresses the "Agile Infrastructure Gap" by providing a rigorous yet accessible methodology for early-phase mechatronic PLE. 

\subsection{Structural Rigor and Maintainability}

One of the most significant advantages of the three-tier separation is the decoupling of variability logic from physical geometry. By isolating atomic assets in Tier 3 (Parts), we ensure that the logical feature model in Tier 2 remains clean. In traditional flat models, geometric changes (e.g., updating a screw size) often require manual updates of configuration rules. In our pattern, a change to a Tier 3 submodel propagates automatically through the hierarchy. This structural rigor allows the product line to scale to hundreds of variants without the "configuration bloat" typically seen in early-phase designs.

\subsection{Portability and the Research Ground Truth}

The reliance on standard LDraw names and hierarchies ensures that the UVR pattern is fully portable. This is particularly relevant for the PLE research community, which lacks shared, reproducible benchmarks for hardware variability. By providing a single-file "150\% ground truth" \cite{Strueber2019}, our approach enables researchers to test derivation and collision detection algorithms on a common mechatronic artifact that does not require proprietary database access. Furthermore, by using digital LEGO as a "low-fidelity, high-rigor" proxy, we lower the barrier for students to experiment with complex PLE concepts, making abstract logical dependencies tangible.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper has presented the Three-Tier UVR, a novel architectural pattern for embedding variability management directly within mechatronic CAD models. By formally separating Configurations, Features, and Parts within a single data structure, we bridge the gap between lightweight design and industrial PLM. Our drone case study demonstrates that this approach is both technically feasible and scientifically rigorous, providing a foundation for managing complex geometric and logical constraints.

Future research will focus on two directions. First, we aim to integrate the UVR convention with the emerging UVL standard \cite{Benavides2025UVL}, creating a bridge between 3D assets and problem-space models. Second, we plan to extend the pattern to manage multi-domain assets, such as firmware code and technical documentation, within the same hierarchical structure. Embedding configuration intelligence directly into the geometry is a vital step toward making mechatronic PLE as agile as its software counterpart.
